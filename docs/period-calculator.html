Your estimate

If your cycles are irregular (for example in perimenopause or with PCOS), a calendar estimate like this is much less reliable. Logging your real period dates and symptoms over several cycles gives a better picture than any formula.

How this estimate works

Your next period is counted forward from the first day of your last period by your usual cycle length. If the last period you entered was a while ago, the calculator keeps adding your cycle length until it reaches the next date that has not passed yet.

Ovulation is estimated at about 14 days before that next period. The NHS says ovulation happens about 12 to 16 days before the start of your next period, so 14 is the middle of that range. The fertile window shown runs from 5 days before the estimated ovulation day to the day itself: womenshealth.gov says sperm can survive 3 to 5 days in the reproductive organs, and the egg lives for only 12 to 24 hours after ovulation. It is a rough guide, not a measurement.

What is a typical cycle?

The NHS says most people have a period around every 28 days, with cycles commonly ranging from every 21 days to every 35 days, and that a period usually lasts about 5 days (anywhere from 2 to 7). Other sources draw the line slightly differently: womenshealth.gov describes 24 to 38 days as a normal cycle. Cycle length is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next.

When to see a doctor

The NHS says a change in your periods does not necessarily mean there is a problem, but it does need to be looked into, so see a GP or a clinic if your periods change. womenshealth.gov lists bleeding that often lasts longer than eight days as a symptom of heavy bleeding. In your 30s and 40s, cycles that become longer or shorter, skipped periods and lighter or heavier flow are common signs of perimenopause, and ACOG still advises reporting changes in menstrual bleeding to a health care professional.

Not medical advice. This page is general information and a simple date calculator. It does not diagnose anything and cannot tell you whether your cycle is healthy. If something is new, severe or worrying, talk to a qualified healthcare professional.

Common questions

Can I use this as birth control?

No. A calendar estimate is not contraception, and this tool only counts days. The NHS says fertility awareness methods, which are far more thorough than counting days, are 91% to 99% effective at preventing pregnancy when used correctly all the time, but only 76% effective if the instructions are not followed exactly, and may not suit you if you have irregular periods.

How accurate is the ovulation estimate?

It is a rough guide. The NHS gives 12 to 16 days between ovulation and your next period, and womenshealth.gov says the gap can be anywhere from 7 days to more than 2 weeks (19 days). The real ovulation day can therefore differ from the estimate by several days, and more so if your cycles vary.

Why did my period come earlier or later than predicted?

Cycle length naturally varies from one cycle to the next, and a calendar formula cannot see what is happening in your body. If your periods keep changing, or you are worried, mention it to a doctor or clinic.

What if my cycles are irregular?

A single cycle length cannot describe an irregular pattern. ACOG notes that in perimenopause cycles may become longer or shorter and you may skip periods. Try your shortest and longest recent cycle in the calculator to see the spread, and log your real period dates and symptoms over several cycles instead of relying on one number.

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. The dates you enter are used only to do the arithmetic in your browser. They are not stored, not sent to a server and not shared, and the page uses no cookies or browser storage.
